2.5.1 Group Project - Encoding of facial features
This exercise is inspired by the papers by Fagertun et al. (Fagertun et al., 2012, 2013) described above.
You can find the papers on DTU Findit. In the exercise, you will run two simple experiments. You will
also create and validate a linear encoding model experimentally. In Experiment 1, the test participants
will view photos of human faces. The participants must rate each face on a 5-step scale to indicate a
facial feature. The facial feature can be the emotional expression of the face (e.g. happy, sad or angry).
It also could be the perceived gender, race or age of the face. It could also be some other feature that
you select.
Using the ratings obtained in Experiment 1, you will create a linear model with the rating as the
dependent variable and the images, or rather, their principal component scores, are the predictors. You
will use the linear model to create synthetic images of faces that correspond to given ratings. Some of
the methods you will use are described in more detail in the Introduction to Machine Learning book
by Herlau, Schmidt and Mørup (Herlau et al., 2021).
In Experiment 2 synthetic images of faces are presented to the test participants. The participants must
rate each face on a scale similar to the one used in Experiment 1. The purpose of Experiment 2 is

3. PCA and dimension reduction
Subtract the average image from all the images. Do not standardise by dividing by the standard
deviation.
Run PCA on the images (with the average image subtracted). The scores (i.e., the representation
of the images in PCA space) will be the predictors in the linear encoding model.
Visually present the first few PCA components as a set of images. Recall, that the principal
components can be interpreted as image components that are added onto the average image,
weighted by the score. One way to visualise the effect of a principal component in the image
is to create three images displayed side-by-side. In the first image add the principal component
weighted by its minimum score to the average image. The second image is simply the average
image. In the third image add the principal component weighted by its maximum score to the
average image. The three images now display how the images vary across the full range of scores
for a single principal component.

6. One common problem that can occur when generating the synthetic images is that the generation
of images extends beyond the data. The problem typically occurs when the facial feature that the
participants rate in the experiment is not very prominent. In this case, the norm of the weight
vector, w, are close to zero. Inspecting Equation 2.22 you can see that the model will predict
that all images are rated by the rating given by the intercept. This is called regression towards
the mean because the intercept reflects the average rating.
You can see from Equation 2.27 that α → ∞ when ∥w∥ → 0. Therefore, the synthetic images will
lie outside of the distribution of the input images where the model fails. The synthetic images
might appear grotesque and may not even resemble faces.
Test whether your synthetic images lie outside the distribution of the input images by calculating
the rating that the model predicts for each of your input images. Find the minimum and maximum
value. This gives you the range of the distribution. Is this the same range as the range that you
are using to generate synthetic images? If not, then generate new synthetic images that falls
within the distribution of predicted ratings.
In your report, describe the he minimum and maximum value for the rating that the model
predicts for the stimuli that you used to train it. Evaluate whether they deviate substantially
from the minimum and maximum ratings that you used to create your stimuli. If they do deviate

8. Conduct Experiment 3. In this experiment, you should test whether your synthetic stimuli can
produce a perceptual after-effect. The adapting stimulus should be a synthetic face located at
one endpoint of the continuum defined by your model. Use each of the two endpoints as adapting
stimuli in two separate adaptation conditions. The test stimuli should lie close to the neutral face.
Use three test stimuli for each adaptation condition, giving a total of six experimental conditions.
Add a fixation point to the centre of every stimulus so that the observer has a fixed point to gaze
at during both the adaptation and test periods.
The structure of each experimental trial should be as follows. First, display the adapting stimulus
for 20–30 seconds. Immediately afterwards, display the test stimulus for 0.5–1.0 seconds. The
test stimulus should be presented only briefly so that the observer judges it while still under the
influence of the adapting stimulus. After the test stimulus has disappeared, prompt the observer
for a response in the same way as in the previous experiment.
The predicted after-effect is that perception of the test stimulus is shifted away from the adapting
stimulus. Thus, the same test face should receive systematically different ratings depending on
which endpoint of the continuum was used for adaptation.
In your report, plot the ratings for the three test stimuli separately for the two adaptation conditions.
For each test stimulus, compare the ratings obtained after adaptation to the two opposite endpoints.
Determine whether the direction of the difference is consistent with the predicted perceptual after-
effect.
